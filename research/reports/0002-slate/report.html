Experiment 0002 · research slate · pre-registered 2026-09-15

All Weather Slate

The systematic method is A4: two hidden Markov chains in place of main's one, a growth chain and an inflation-and-rates chain. Of the six pre-registered ideas it has the largest one-year difference over main, +0.0590 in Brier skill score, and it clears the family-wise bar: its 98.33% interval is [+0.0147, +0.1019]. Its independent look-ahead review is clean, and two further seeds, fixed before they ran, reproduce it.

In All Weather's terms, the frame survives as two axes, not as a grid. Imposing the four quadrants as the state space (A2) also helps at one year. Defining them over surprises, as Dalio's own sources do (A3), does not. Modelling growth and inflation as separate processes, each moving at its own speed, helps most. Nothing combines with A4 under the rule written before the results: no stackable layer qualified, and a model has only one state space.

This is the strongest claim the design allows, and no stronger. The result is in sample: every arm was designed by someone who had already seen main's results. The only out-of-sample test is forecasts registered forward, the first of which resolves on 2027-07-01. Nothing has been merged or shipped; that is your call, below.

Six ideas, one rule

Each arm is one idea, built on its own branch and scored against main on the same 391 forecast dates from 1994-03, the first date the model is honest about. The statistic is the paired difference in mean Brier skill score, arm minus main, with a moving-block bootstrap over forecast dates (10,000 resamples, seed 20260908). The rule was committed before any arm ran: promising if the one-year 90% interval is above zero; confirmed in sample if the one-year 98.33% interval is (1 − 0.10/6, a Bonferroni correction for six arms); harmful if any horizon's 90% interval is below −0.02; void if the look-ahead audit fails or the independent review finds a leak.

What each arm found

A1 · Sticky prior on the transition matrix

No verdict

research/sticky-dirichlet-prior · 1 year +0.0046 · 90% [−0.0126, +0.0232] · review clean

What changed. A Dirichlet prior on each row of the transition matrix, pulling toward staying in the current regime, at a strength fixed at registration. Main's fit declares some regime switches effectively impossible, with transition probabilities as small as 1e-184 to 1e-299. Under the prior, the smallest is about 0.0014, per the reviewer's measurement.

What happened. The one-year interval straddles zero at both levels, so there is no verdict. That is the outcome the pre-registration named as the most likely for any single arm. Persistence fell, as predicted. The ten-year difference is large, but ten-year evidence is about 2.2 observations.

Review: clean. With the prior switched off, the reviewer reproduced main's cached 1994-03 fit bit for bit on real data. It left two notes, neither of them a leak:

  • The report's test count (500) cannot be reproduced; the committed tree has 489 tests.
  • A code comment claims a monotonicity guarantee that holds for the mode update, not for the posterior-mean update the arm actually uses.

A2 · All Weather quadrants over levels, four states

Confirmed in sample

research/quadrant-structure-levels · 1 year +0.0381 · 90% [+0.0144, +0.0583] · review clean

What changed. All Weather's growth-by-inflation grid, imposed on the state space.

  • Every fit has four states, each started from the centroid of one quadrant.
  • The quadrants are split at each series' expanding median, so no threshold is written down anywhere.
  • The burn-in sweep still runs, and would have chosen six states; it now chooses nothing.

What happened. Confirmed in sample at one year, and its five-year interval is above zero too. That is the opposite of its registered prediction, which expected one-year skill to fall with fewer states. It is second to A4. Both arms replace the state space, so only one can be used, and 0003's rule picks the one with the higher one-year difference.

Four runs, one scored.

  • An early run ended with a truncated log.
  • The next was voided, because its code was edited while it ran.
  • The author discarded a third after committing further tests.
  • The scored run started on a clean tree and stayed clean. It gives the same one-year numbers as the voided run to four decimals, so the edits never touched a forecast.

What its audit did not test. The deterministic audit passed, but it fitted main's six states, not A2's four-state seeding (D15). So A2's defence against leaks rests on its independent review.

Review: clean, and it closed the audit's gap by hand. The reviewer confirmed that the recorded audit never ran A2's code, then ran the checks that did:

  • The same audit, forced onto four states, with every fit quadrant-seeded: it passed, with all 2,190 rows identical.
  • Its own perturbation checks at six cutoffs, from 2005 to today: every earlier fit and centroid came out byte-identical.
  • All 34 models refitted with no cache, and the quadrant centroids recomputed by its own loop: both bit-identical to what the scored run used.
  • The recession-lag leak (D14) barely touches this arm: dropping the 8 exposed dates moves its one-year difference only in the fourth decimal.

A3 · All Weather quadrants over surprises

No verdict

research/quadrant-structure-surprises · 1 year +0.0184 · 90% [−0.0050, +0.0404] · review clean

What changed. The model reads surprises instead of levels. Each month's growth and inflation has a recursive AR(1) forecast subtracted, fitted only on earlier months, which is how All Weather's primary sources define the quadrants. The state count is fixed at four.

What happened. No verdict at one year. At five years it came closer to the harm floor than any other arm without crossing it. It is faithful to the source, and no better than main.

Review: clean. The reviewer's checks:

  • The recursive fit agrees with separate regressions on real data, to 9e-15.
  • The arm's tests caught two deliberately leaky copies of the code.
  • The audit alone would miss one version of this leak: a single regression over each date's own panel. The arm's invariance test is what guards that case.
  • Notebooks 02 and 04 still draw this model on the level matrix. That gives a wrong chart, not a leak, and matters only if the arm ships.

A4 · Two chains: growth, and inflation with rates

Confirmed in sample

research/two-timescale-chains · 1 year +0.0590 · 90% [+0.0275, +0.0877] · review clean

What changed. Two independent hidden Markov chains replace main's one: a growth chain, and an inflation-and-rates chain.

  • The joint regime is the pair: sixteen joint regimes, chosen as four by four on the burn-in panel that ends on 1994-01-01.
  • Filtering is main's own forward pass.
  • The fit is exact expectation maximisation, because the likelihood separates by chain.

What happened. It has the largest one-year difference of the six arms, and its one-year 98.33% interval is above zero, and it also improves at five and ten years. Under experiment 0001's rule, its one-year verdict flips from the base rate to the model. At every refit, the joint persistence equals the slower chain's, as predicted.

Two further seeds, fixed before they ran, reproduce it. That rules out a lucky starting point as the explanation (see Is A4 just a lucky seed? below).

Review: clean.

  • Both state counts were chosen before the first forecast date.
  • Forecasts use filtered probabilities. The arm's test fails, by 0.396, if smoothed ones are swapped in.
  • The arm's regimes-exist gate used a restricted null. Against main's full-covariance null on the same panel, the reviewer measured the gap at 1.81 per month in held-out log likelihood. The easier null accounts for 0.08 of it.

Caveats that travel with it.

  • It changes the structure and the state count at once: 16 joint states against 6, with fewer free parameters (58 against 89). So the gain cannot be attributed to the timescale split alone.
  • Each forecast rests on thinner evidence: about 45 to 48 months of effective sample, against 112 to 122 for main.

A5 · Direct per-horizon regime rates

Harmful

research/direct-horizon-rates · 1 year −0.1017 · 90% [−0.1448, −0.0586] · review clean

What changed. Each horizon's forecast normally comes from one-month rates composed through the transition matrix. This arm instead estimates each regime's rate of the h-horizon outcome directly, from months whose outcome had resolved and been published by the refit date, with main's shrinkage. The benchmark and the new rates share one implementation of the publication boundary.

What happened. Harmful at one year, so it is disqualified from any combination.

Review: clean, and it found D14.

  • The reviewer rebuilt all 11,730 backtest probabilities from the registered formula, with its own date arithmetic, and every one matched.
  • Checking the recession series' lag against NBER's announcements, it found the main-level leak now recorded as D14.
  • It traced this arm's odd ten-year interval to the bootstrap, which every arm shares. With 120-month blocks over 270 dates, 4,557 of 10,000 resamples are refused because a rare outcome is missing from them.

A6 · Fixed 50/50 blend with climatology

Void

research/fixed-climatology-blend · 1 year −0.0209 · 90% [−0.0697, +0.0296] · review leak found

What changed. Pure post-processing, with the model unchanged. Every forecast becomes half the model's probability and half the climatology's, at every horizon and for every indicator. There is no fitted weight, because the research showed that fitting one on about 31 outcomes does harm.

What happened. Its numbers alone give no verdict at one year, and it is not harmful. Pulling halfway to climatology gives back some of the model's one-year skill. It helps most at ten years, where main's skill is negative, as the registration predicted, but ten-year evidence is uninformative. The blend was the combination competitions' most robust result, and here it does not beat the model at the horizon that matters.

The prediction that failed. One-year calibration error was registered to fall. By the only measure the tooling produces, the arm's report finds it rose slightly.

Review: leak found, so the arm is void under the rule. The arm's code does what the registration says; the leak is in the registered design. Main's climatology is clean only because main's forecasts never read it. Blending it into the forecast gives two known main-level approximations a way into the forecast:

  • The recession lag (D14). Post-trough labels read before NBER's announcements move the blended forecast by at most 0.00083, on exactly D14's eight dates.
  • Revised values (D5). The climatology averages outcomes from today's revised data, back to 1919, and main's forecasts have no such channel. Rebuilt from archival vintages, industrial production moves the blend by up to 0.0047, and the one-year difference moves by 0.0002.

Neither could move a verdict. The pre-registration voids an arm for any such path, not only a large one, and the rule is applied as written.

Everything else checked clean:

  • There is one publication boundary, not two.
  • The carry-forward never looks ahead and never reaches a scored row.
  • The model parameters are identical to main's.

The arm's own report is wrong on two points:

  • It says no test assertion was loosened, but one was: bool became bool | float. The change was needed for the new setting and hides nothing.
  • It calls the audit an end-to-end check of the blend. The audit sees neither leak channel.

Is A4 just a lucky seed?

A4 fits sixteen joint states by expectation maximisation from random starting points, so one lucky local optimum could produce a result this size. Before either variant ran, diagnostic 0004 fixed how to read two more seeds, the next two integers after the registered one: robust to initialisation if both keep the one-year 90% interval above zero, and seed-dependent if either does not. The diagnostic cannot change A4's registered verdict. It decides how strongly the result can be stated, and whether A4 can be recommended.

What the checks caught

The most important findings of this slate are not arms. They are leaks and defects that would have mis-scored everything, and each was caught by a check rather than by someone reading the code.

Main had a look-ahead, found the first time the audit ran. The climatology benchmark counted each outcome h months after its forecast date, before the observation it rests on was published: 32 to 400 days early. The pre-registration had said the slate halts until main is clean. It halted, main was fixed (ADR 0009), and the harness measured the effect: one-year skill +0.2128 → +0.2154, paired interval [+0.0021, +0.0028]. The leak flattered the benchmark, not the model, and no verdict moved.

A second, smaller one, found by a reviewer where the audit cannot look (technical debt D14). The registry says a recession month is known 400 days after it. NBER announces turning points, not months: every peak since 1990 was announced within 400 days (128 to 366), and every trough after them (474 to 662, all eight dates checked at nber.org). So the months just after each trough reached the benchmark and the rates early, on 8 of the 391 scored dates, for the two recession indicators only. The audit trusts registered lags, so it could not see this. Main and every arm read the same registry, so it cannot favour an arm. Fixing it is deferred until this slate closes, so the reference stays put.

Defects caught before they could mis-score anything:

  • The runner only asked for the 90% interval. No arm could have reached confirmed, and each report would have recorded that as a result.
  • A reviewed fix that was never wired in. ADR 0007's likelihood allowance was tested but never called. It is now wired in, and full recomputation of 35 fits and 11,730 rows proved it byte-identical.
  • The run summary borrowed its fallback count from another command's file, which would have given every arm a spurious categorical change.
  • The audit's own gap. It perturbed by label, not by publication date, so the classic one-month look-ahead inside a publication lag would have passed. It was closed with a test that the old rule passes and the new one fails.
  • The scorer's review parser, three times. A substring search would have voided a clean arm whose reviewer wrote "no leak found". A regex emptied every section. And the first real review titled itself with the arm's name, which the parser took as the arm's section and read as unreadable. Each failed safe, and fixtures now pin all three.
  • A run whose code was being edited while it ran. One arm's exit_codes.json named a commit whose code never ran whole. The runner now refuses a dirty tree and records whether it stayed clean. The scorer refuses any arm whose worktree differs from its run's commit, and it applied that to earlier runs too.
  • The audit tested one arm with main's state count. Its from-scratch run chooses the number of regimes by calling the sweep directly. The backtest decides it one layer up, and that is where A2 fixes four states. So A2's audit fitted six states and never ran A2's seeding. A2's own author found it after the run, and it is verified in the code. The audit logs show that every other arm's audit fitted the arm's own count, so only A2 was affected (D15).
  • A leak by design, in the blend arm. Main's benchmark is clean as a benchmark, because no forecast reads it. A6's reviewer found that blending it into the forecast gives D14 and the revised-values approximation (D5) a way into forecasts. The leak is tiny: at most 0.0047 on one forecast, and 0.0002 on the one-year difference. The rule voids A6 anyway, as written, and the debt table's benchmark row now carries the warning.
  • An arm stopped, correctly, before writing code. A6's brief said to stop if the climatology was ever missing, and it was missing at today's date. The cause was index coverage, never a scored row, and it was resolved with the rule submit() already uses.

What the research says

Three briefings in research/briefings/ fed the slate's design. Each number below was derived or fetched, not quoted from memory.

  • The one-year calibration gate cannot be passed reliably at this sample size. A perfectly calibrated forecaster fails experiment 0001's ten-bin calibration-error gate (below 0.10) about 91% of the time at n = 31, the honest one-year sample. The threshold was not moved. That is your decision, below.
  • Fitted recalibration on about 31 outcomes makes forecasts worse out of sample. Platt, beta and isotonic maps all did, which is why the blend arm uses fixed weights.
  • The model is not a business-cycle model. Its second eigenvalue, about 0.98, matches the published volatility and level chains, not the growth chains (0.83 to 0.93). Output growth fitted alone runs about three times faster, with a half-life of 12 months against 25 to 43 for inflation and rates. That was the motivation for A4.
  • The information horizon is algebra, not bad luck. Hamilton (eq. 3–4) and Krolzig (eq. 21) show that regime forecasts converge to the unconditional mean.
  • All Weather's primary sources define the quadrants over surprises, not levels. The widely quoted 55/30/15 split appears in neither primary document. Hence two quadrant arms: A2 over levels and A3 over surprises.
  • External yardsticks. Professional forecasters' recession probabilities beat climatology one quarter ahead (+0.13) but not three to four quarters ahead. A real-time yield-curve model scores about +0.06, and is negative after 1990.

What this does not show

  • Every arm was designed by someone who had seen main's results. Pre-registration closes the tuning path but not the idea-selection path, so confirmed in sample is the ceiling here. Out-of-sample confirmation comes only from forecasts registered forward, the first resolving on 2027-07-01.
  • The look-ahead audit trusts the registry's publication lags (D14 is the proof that this matters). Revised values (D5) remain an accepted approximation. And it sees information on or after its cutoff, not between an earlier forecast and the cutoff.
  • Ten-year evidence is about 2.2 independent observations. No ten-year verdict here is informative, and the ten-year bootstrap rejects a large share of its resamples because rare outcomes sit in rarely drawn dates.
  • ADR 0007's other promise, that a fall in likelihood abandons one restart rather than the whole fit, is still not true in code.
  • The machine hibernated for 4 h 44 min mid-slate (16:00 to 20:44). Every run paused and resumed. No number depends on it, and it is recorded because it looked like a hang.
