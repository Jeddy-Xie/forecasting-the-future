## Executive summary
1. What wins is dull: base rates, equal-weight combination, avoiding overconfidence. In M6 only 23% of teams beat an equal-probability benchmark, by <2.5%; the average of top teams beat the best team (W1, W2).
2. Superforecaster skill is real and persistent but mostly discrimination, not calibration; <1 h of training bought 6–11% Brier, an effect a 2024 reanalysis contests (W3).
3. Extremizing corrects under-aggregated human crowds; there is no evidence for applying it to a single statistical model (W4, P4).
4. Derived: at ~31 independent outcomes, Platt, beta and isotonic recalibration worsen out-of-sample Brier even for a truly miscalibrated model; only a strong prior toward identity is safe, gaining ~0.002 (W5).
5. Derived: a perfectly calibrated forecaster fails ECE < 0.10 ~91% of the time at n = 31, so the current gate failure is not evidence of miscalibration. Verdict and threshold stand; report the ECE null distribution and take this to Jeddy (T1).
6. Derived: SPF recession probabilities beat climatology only one quarter ahead; the real-time yield-curve probit has negative 12-month skill after 1990. Climatology is a hard one-year bar (W7).
7. Brier forgives confident errors on rare events (3.4% vs 20.8% excess under log score, 5%-vs-1% case), and mean BSS is dominated by lopsided indicators (W6).
8. Best next step: pre-register an equal-weight model/climatology blend, plus the two external references, as a successor experiment (T3, T4).

## Well-evidenced

### W1. In the closest analogue (M6, financial), almost nobody beat the equal-probability benchmark, and the margin was tiny
Source: Makridakis, Spiliotis, Hollyman, Petropoulos, Swanson & Gaba, "The M6 forecasting competition:
Bridging the gap between forecasting and investment decisions", arXiv:2310.13357 (20 Oct 2023),
https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13357 — full PDF fetched and read.
- Task: probabilities that each of 100 assets lands in each return quintile, 12 live monthly rounds, scored by
  ranked probability score (RPS). The benchmark gives 0.2 to every quintile and scores RPS = 0.16.
- Of 163 teams on the global leaderboard, **38 (23.3%) beat the benchmark on RPS**; those that did "improved the
  accuracy by less than 2.5%"; **only 3 teams beat it in all 12 months**, fewer than 15 in more than 9.
  Participants "had unwarranted certainty ... thus being overconfident in general."
- Forecast accuracy and investment return were essentially uncorrelated (RPS vs IR, r = 0.04 across teams).
- What reliably helped: **averaging the forecasts of top teams "consistently outperform[ed] the forecasts made by
  the best performing participant", and "said combinations always outperform the benchmark."** All five
  forecasting-track winners resubmitted at every round.
- Organisers' own caveat: one year is too short for "'skill' [to be] effectively separated from 'luck'";
  investment winners "may have benefited from 'luck' ... little evidence ... their positive IRs were generated by
  superior investment insights."
- Independent post-mortem: Staněk, "M6 Investment Challenge: The Role of Luck and Strategic Considerations",
  arXiv:2412.04490 (Nov 2024), full text read: "insufficient evidence to suggest that the extreme Sharpe ratios
  observed are beyond what one would expect by chance, given the number of teams" (Wright-Yam-Yung test of equal
  expected Sharpe ratios not rejected), and top rank can be pursued by adversarial risk-taking unrelated to skill.
- **Transfer:** in a near-efficient target, an equal-probability/climatological reference is very hard to beat,
  the achievable gain is a few percent of score, and the typical failure is overconfidence. This is exactly the
  structure of this project's decision rule (BSS threshold 0.02 against climatology).

### W2. Combining forecasts beats picking the best one (M4, M5, M6 — repeatedly)
- M6 (W1): the average of the top teams beat the single best team and always beat the benchmark.
Source: Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos, "The M5 Accuracy competition: Results, findings and conclusions"
(preprint Oct 2020, published IJF 2022), https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344487258 (PDF read). The M4
results paper itself (IJF 36(1), 2020, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169207019301128) returned
HTTP 403 and is listed under Could not verify; the M4 statements below come from the M5 paper's own summary.
- M4: the winner was a hybrid of exponential smoothing and recurrent nets (Smyl); second place used XGBoost to
  *weight standard statistical forecasts*; "both ... were similarly accurate to a simple combination (median) of
  four statistical methods."
- M5 "Finding 2: The value of combining" — winner = "very simple, equal-weighted combination, involving 6 models";
  runner-up equal-weighted over 5; third an equal-weighted combination of 43 NNs. Only one of the combination
  entries among the top 50 used unequal weights.
- M5 "Finding 3": cross-learning (one model trained across many related series) beat series-by-series fitting.
- **Transfer:** equal weights beat estimated weights when the evaluation sample is small — the "forecast
  combination puzzle". With ~31 independent one-year outcomes, estimated combination weights will be noise;
  equal-weight (or fixed a-priori) combination is the default that competitions support.

### W3. The Good Judgment Project: individual skill is real, persistent and partly trainable — with measured sizes
- Mellers et al. (2014), "Psychological Strategies for Winning a Geopolitical Forecasting Tournament",
  Psychological Science 25(5), doi:10.1177/0956797614524255; author copy https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4rg4n9vr
  (read). 199 questions, ~1,600 forecasters/yr. Training "corrected cognitive biases, encouraged forecasters to use
  reference classes, and provided ... heuristics, such as averaging when multiple estimates were available."
  Training effect Year 1: F(2,1586)=14.29, p<.001; teaming F(2,1586)=60.68, p<.001. Training, teaming and tracking
  "improved both calibration and resolution." Early 100% forecasts (first 20% of a question's life) came true only
  ~70% of the time; the same forecasts in the last 20% of days ~90% — i.e. early extreme confidence is the error.
- Chang, Chen, Mellers & Tetlock (2016), "Developing expert political judgment", Judgment and Decision Making
  11(5):509–526, https://jbaron.org/journal/16/16511/jdm16511.pdf (read): a training module of **under one hour
  "consistently improved accuracy (Brier scores) by 6 to 11% over the control condition"** across four tournament
  years. This is the cleanest randomised effect size in the literature.
- Mellers et al. (2015), "Identifying and Cultivating Superforecasters...", Perspectives on Psychological Science
  10(3):267–281, doi:10.1177/1745691615577794 (read). Top-2% performers placed in elite teams "defying expectations
  of regression toward the mean 2 years in a row". Table 1 (avg of Years 2–3), superforecasters vs all others:
  standardised Brier −0.34 vs +0.04; AUC 96% vs 75%; calibration error 0.01 vs 0.04; resolution 0.40 vs 0.32.
  **Note the decomposition: most of the gap is resolution/discrimination, not calibration.**
- Atanasov, Witkowski, Ungar, Mellers & Tetlock (2020), "Small steps to accuracy: Incremental belief updaters are
  better forecasters", OBHDP 160:19–35, doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.02.001 (read): >400,000 forecasts on ~500
  questions; "the most accurate forecasters made frequent, small updates", low-skill forecasters confirmed or made
  "infrequent, large revisions"; update magnitude mediated the causal effect of training.
- **Contested:** Hauenstein, Thomas, Illingworth & Dougherty (2024), "Rethinking the Role of Teams and Training in
  Geopolitical Forecasting", Psychological Science 36(1):3–18, doi:10.1177/09567976241266481 (DOI resolves;
  abstract read from workspace copy): an item-response reanalysis of the Mellers et al. data in which extraneous
  variables "substantially eliminated, reduced, and, in some cases, even reversed the effects of the experimental
  manipulations of teaming and training". Treat the 6–11% training effect as probable, not settled.
- Transfer caveat: these are human-judgment effects on short-fuse geopolitical questions (average open ~102 days).
  What transfers to an automated system is the *method* (reference classes, averaging, small updates, avoiding early
  extremes), not the effect sizes.

### W4. Aggregation: averaging in log-odds, then extremizing, helps when forecasters hold partly independent information
- Baron, Mellers, Tetlock, Stone & Ungar (2014), "Two Reasons to Make Aggregated Probability Forecasts More
  Extreme", Decision Analysis 11(2):133–145, doi:10.1287/deca.2014.0293 (full text read from a copy fetched in
  this workspace; the publisher URL returns 403 to the fetcher). Transform used: t(p) = p^a / (p^a + (1−p)^a),
  with a = 2.5 in their Figure 1; "the optimal values of a were greater than 1" in GJP data. Two causes: (i) the
  mean of probabilities is pulled toward 0.5 by the bounded scale (does not affect median or mean log-odds);
  (ii) each forecaster sees only part of the evidence, so the crowd is collectively underconfident (affects all).
- Satopää, Jensen, Mellers, Tetlock & Ungar (2014), "Probability aggregation in time-series: dynamic hierarchical
  modeling of sparse expert beliefs", Annals of Applied Statistics 8(2):1256–1280, arXiv:1408.0087 (read): all
  expertise groups were **underconfident** at group level; a model calibrated in-sample by maximising log score
  (BSAC-LOG) "is well calibrated in-sample but presents overconfidence out-of-sample" — direct evidence that fitted
  calibration overfits.
- Neyman & Roughgarden (2022), "Are You Smarter Than a Random Expert? The Robust Aggregation of Substitutable
  Signals", arXiv:2111.03153 (read): proves that averaging then extremizing away from the prior by a constant
  factor improves worst-case guarantees, **but** "if the signals are fully correlated ... it is optimal not to
  extremize; in the second [independent], it is optimal to extremize a lot."
- **Transfer (important):** extremizing corrects for *many partially-informed humans*. A single statistical model
  has no crowd to under-aggregate; its miscalibration can go either way. Extremizing this system's output is
  justified only if its measured reliability curve is too flat — and see W5 on whether that can be measured.
- Metaculus's own experience (FAQ, https://www.metaculus.com/faq/, read): the Community Prediction is a
  recency-weighted median; the fancier calibrated-and-weighted "Metaculus Prediction" is **deprecated since
  November 2024**; for 2021-resolved questions it scored Brier 0.107 vs 0.108 for the plain Community Prediction
  — i.e. the sophisticated recalibration bought 0.001 of Brier.

### W5. Post-hoc recalibration on ~31–50 independent observations does not help out of sample — and the ECE gate cannot be passed reliably at that n even by a perfect forecaster  [E2: derived here]
Literature:
- Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana (2005), "Predicting Good Probabilities With Supervised Learning", ICML,
  https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~alexn/papers/calibration.icml05.crc.rev3.pdf (read): "When the calibration set is
  small (less than about 200-1000 cases), Platt Scaling outperforms Isotonic Regression"; isotonic "is less
  constrained ... so it is easier for it to overfit"; Platt "also has some overfitting" at small sizes.
  Their learning curves start at ~10 calibration cases, so this project's regime (~31) is at the bottom edge of what they tested.
- scikit-learn user guide, https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html (read): isotonic "is more
  prone to overfitting, especially on small datasets"; matches sigmoid only with ">~1000 samples".
- Kull, Silva Filho & Flach (2017), "Beta calibration", AISTATS, PMLR 54, https://proceedings.mlr.press/v54/kull17a.html
  (read): logistic calibration "does not include the identity function, and hence ... can easily uncalibrate a
  perfectly calibrated classifier"; beta map = sigmoid(a·ln p − b·ln(1−p) + c), 3 parameters.
Derived (seeded simulation, `scratchpad/sim_calibration.py`, seed 20260915; true probabilities logit-normal with
base rate ~0.28, 10 equal-width bins, same ECE formula as `evaluation/calibration.py`; recalibrators fitted on n
observations and scored on 40,000 fresh ones, 1,000 repetitions):

ECE of a **perfectly calibrated** forecaster:

| independent n | median ECE | 90th pct | P(ECE < 0.10) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 31 | 0.153 | 0.211 | **0.09** |
| 50 | 0.124 | 0.170 | 0.23 |
| 62 | 0.111 | 0.153 | 0.35 |
| 100 | 0.088 | 0.121 | 0.68 |
| 155 | 0.071 | 0.098 | 0.91 |
| 310 | 0.051 | 0.070 | 1.00 |

Change in out-of-sample Brier from recalibrating (negative = helps; "hurt" = share of repetitions made worse):

| true miscalibration | n | Platt | ridge-Platt (prior = identity) | beta | isotonic | shrink-to-base-rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| none | 31 | +0.012 (hurt 100%) | +0.003 (100%) | +0.021 | +0.023 | +0.003 (45%) |
| overconfident, logit slope 1.6 | 31 | +0.007 (59%) | **−0.002 (24%)** | +0.017 | +0.017 | +0.002 (24%) |
| overconfident + biased | 31 | +0.006 (56%) | −0.001 (32%) | +0.014 | +0.017 | −0.001 (16%) |
| underconfident, slope 0.6 | 31 | +0.004 (50%) | −0.004 (12%) | +0.013 | +0.015 | +0.003 (30%) |
| overconfident, slope 1.6 | 100 | −0.002 (23%) | −0.003 (8%) | +0.000 | +0.005 | −0.001 (12%) |
| overconfident, slope 1.6 | 300 | −0.005 (1%) | −0.005 (0%) | −0.004 | −0.001 | −0.002 (2%) |

Read-off:
1. At n ≈ 31, **unregularised Platt, beta and isotonic recalibration make out-of-sample Brier worse on average
   even when the model really is miscalibrated in exactly the logistic form Platt assumes.** Isotonic is worst.
   Break-even for plain Platt is around n ≈ 50–100; isotonic needs several hundred.
2. Only recalibration with a strong prior toward the identity map (ridge-Platt; penalty fixed a priori at 5, not
   tuned) is safe at n ≈ 31, and its gain is ~0.002 Brier (about 1% of the Brier score).
3. **A 10-bin ECE threshold of 0.10 is failed by a perfectly calibrated forecaster ~91% of the time at 31
   independent observations** (median ECE 0.153). Binned ECE is biased upward at small n because |gap| is
   always positive. If the gate's effective n is ~31, failing it is not evidence of miscalibration.
   Caveat: this assumes the ECE's sampling noise tracks the effective number of independent outcomes. ECE is
   invariant to exact replication of observations, so twelve overlapping monthly forecasts sharing one outcome
   window behave closer to one observation than to twelve; the true noise sits between the n_eff and raw-n rows.
   This is a measured property of the statistic, not a proposal to move the threshold — see T1.

### W6. Scoring rules: Brier is forgiving of confident errors on rare events; skill scores are not strictly proper at small n  [published + E2 arithmetic]
- Gneiting & Raftery (2007), "Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation", JASA 102(477):359–378,
  https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf (read). "Skill scores ... are
  generally improper, even if the underlying scoring rule S is proper"; the Brier skill score is only
  "asymptotically proper, in the sense that the benefits of hedging become negligible as the number of independent
  forecasts grows" (Murphy 1973). The log score "has been criticized for its unboundedness"; every proper *local*
  rule is equivalent to the log score (Bernardo 1979).
- Metaculus scores FAQ, https://www.metaculus.com/help/scores-faq/ (workspace copy): the log score is "much more
  punitive of extreme wrong predictions than it is rewarding of extreme right predictions" — moving 99%→99.9% gains
  +0.009 if right, costs −2.3 if wrong.
- scikit-learn guide (above): "A lower Brier loss ... does not necessarily mean a better calibrated model".
Derived (plain arithmetic):

| situation | Brier | log score (nats) |
|---|---|---|
| wrong at 0.5 / 0.9 / 0.99 / 0.999 | 0.25 / 0.81 / 0.98 / 0.998 | 0.69 / 2.30 / 4.61 / 6.91 |
| true 5%, forecast 1% (5× overconfident) — expected excess as share of climatology's score | **3.4%** | **20.8%** |
| true 2%, forecast 0.1% | 1.8% | 41.9% |
| true 5%, forecast 20% (4× underconfident) | 47.4% | 47.3% |

| base rate | climatological Brier | BSS change from a 5-point bias (+0.0025 Brier) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 0.250 | −0.010 |
| 0.20 | 0.160 | −0.016 |
| 0.05 | 0.0475 | −0.053 |
| 0.02 | 0.0196 | −0.128 |

Read-off:
1. Brier barely notices a forecaster who is 5–20× too confident that a rare event will not happen (the error
   that matters for "inflation above 5%" or "policy rate below 1%"). The log score does. Brier is the registered
   metric and should stay so; the log score is a diagnostic to report beside it.
2. BSS is a ratio whose denominator is p(1−p). For indicators that are rare or near-certain at a given horizon (e.g.
   "unemployment above 5% at some point within ten years"), the same absolute error moves BSS 5–13× more than for
   a 50/50 indicator. **A mean of BSS across ten indicators is therefore dominated by its most lopsided
   indicators.** Report each indicator's climatological Brier next to its BSS so a reader can see this.
3. At n_eff ≈ 31 the BSS is not strictly proper (Gneiting & Raftery): a system could in principle gain by hedging
   toward climatology. That is one more reason to keep the model's output un-tuned against the evaluation window.

### W7. Macro benchmarks: professional recession probabilities have skill only at very short horizons, and the famous yield-curve model does not reliably beat expanding climatology in real time after 1990  [published + E2 derived here]
Published:
- Rudebusch & Williams, "Forecasting Recessions: The Puzzle of the Enduring Power of the Yield Curve", FRBSF
  Working Paper 2007-16 (July 2008; JBES 2009), https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/wp07-16bk.pdf (read).
  Target: a quarter of negative real GDP growth ("R1"). SPF probabilities are "somewhat better" than a real-time
  yield-spread probit in the current quarter (not significant at 5%); **at 3–4 quarters the yield-spread probit is
  significantly more accurate than the SPF**, and the pattern held in the post-1987 subsample ("economists in
  the survey failed to 'learn'"). Table 1, first-final data, 4 quarters ahead: RMSE 0.342 (SPF) vs 0.311 (spread),
  i.e. Brier = RMSE² = 0.117 vs 0.097.
- Estrella & Mishkin, "The Yield Curve as a Predictor of U.S. Recessions", FRBNY Current Issues 2(7), June 1996
  (read from a workspace copy): 10-year minus 3-month Treasury spread in a probit "significantly outperforms other
  financial and macroeconomic indicators in predicting recessions two to six quarters ahead."
- Estrella & Trubin, "The Yield Curve as a Leading Indicator: Some Practical Issues", FRBNY Current Issues 12(5),
  July/Aug 2006, https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/current_issues/ci12-5.pdf (read). This is
  the NY Fed model: P(recession in month t+12) = Φ(−0.6045 − 0.7374 × spread_t), spread = 10-year CMT minus
  3-month bill (bond-equivalent), fitted Jan 1959–Dec 2005. Monthly-average inversion preceded all six recessions
  1968–2005 with "no false signals over this period" — **all in-sample**. The NY Fed chart PDF
  (https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/capital_markets/Prob_Rec.pdf, fetched) shows the
  spread series to Aug 2026 (0.87 pp).
- SPF Anxious Index page, https://www.philadelphiafed.org/surveys-and-data/real-time-data-research/anxious-index
  (read): probability of a real-GDP decline in the quarter after the survey, from 1968:Q4; 2026:Q3 reading 20.0%.
  RECESS1–5 definitions confirmed in the SPF documentation PDF (fetched from the Philadelphia Fed SPF page).

Derived here (`scratchpad/macro_benchmarks.py`; data: Philadelphia Fed SPF `prob.xlsx` RECESS sheet, FRED GDPC1
current vintage, USREC, GS10, TB3MS, all fetched 2026-09-15; bootstrap = moving block, 5,000 draws, seed 20260915;
climatology = expanding mean of outcomes already resolved and, for NBER dates, announced — 13-month lag,
mirroring the project's 400-day rule):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| SPF RECESS3 | decline in 2 quarters | 1968–2026 | 229 | +0.030 | [−0.064, +0.115] | 0.073 | 0.96 |
| SPF RECESS4 | decline in 3 quarters | 1968–2026 | 228 | −0.026 | [−0.096, +0.032] | 0.049 | 0.42 |

| real-time expanding probit on spread | NBER recession in month t+12 | 1975–2025 | 608 mo | +0.060 | [−0.275, +0.306] | 0.042 | 0.56 |
| same | same | 1990–2025 | 428 mo | −0.103 | [−0.719, +0.220] | 0.057 | 0.49 |

| NY Fed published coefficients | recession in month t+12 | 2006–2025 (out of sample for them) | 236 mo | −0.274 | [−1.262, +0.118] | 0.086 | 0.26 |

Read-off:
1. **Professional forecasters' probabilities of a GDP decline beat climatology only one quarter ahead** (BSS
   ≈ +0.13, interval just excluding zero). At 3–4 quarters they are *worse* than climatology, and their
   calibration slope collapses (0.42, then −0.18): the variation in their numbers carries no usable information
   a year out. Their *average* level is fine (ECE 0.05–0.07 on n ≈ 230) — they fail on resolution, not calibration.
2. The yield-curve probit — the standard "beyond climatology" benchmark — earns positive skill over the full
   1975–2025 sample (+0.06 to +0.13 depending on target), but **its skill is negative in real time after 1990 and
   strongly negative after 2006**, driven by the 2022–24 inversion that was not followed by an NBER recession.
   Every interval spans zero. Calibration slopes of 0.2–0.56 mean the probit is overconfident out of sample.
3. So the honest bar for this system at one year on the recession indicator is: **the expanding climatology is
   itself a hard benchmark, and the best-known single-indicator model does not beat it reliably.** A mean BSS of
   +0.02 with a bootstrap interval excluding zero (the pre-registered skill gate) would exceed what either
   professional forecasters or the yield-curve model achieved at this horizon in post-1990 data.
Caveats: current-vintage GDP (Rudebusch & Williams show first-final vs advance matters); bill rate from TB3MS is
on a discount basis, not bond-equivalent as in the NY Fed series; the SPF target (one quarter of GDP decline) is
not the project's NBER-based target. These change levels, not the qualitative pattern.

## Plausible but unproven

- **P1. Good Judgment Inc.'s headline numbers.** Karvetski, "Superforecasters: A Decade of Stochastic Dominance",
  Good Judgment technical white paper, Oct 2021 (workspace copy): on 108 questions shared with GJ Open,
  Superforecasters were 35.9% more accurate (Brier) than GJ Open forecasters, their aggregate 25.1% better than the
  GJ Open aggregate, miscalibration 0.014 vs 0.068; also asserts they "were over 30% more accurate than US
  intelligence analysts forecasting the same events with access to classified information". Company white paper,
  commercial interest, no independent replication; the intelligence-analyst comparison cites no primary source in
  the text read. E1 at best.
- **P2. Superforecaster identification in small pools.** Katsagounos, Thomakos, Litsiou & Nikolopoulos,
  "Superforecasting reality check: Evidence from a small pool of experts and expedited identification", IJF,
  PMC7333631 (workspace copy; intro and conclusion read): 314 experts, 9-month identification window, extra
  20-minute training. The magnitude of the result was not extracted, so it is recorded as a question: selection
  on a short track record is where luck and skill get confounded.
- **P3. Aggregates beat almost all individuals; individual top finishes regress.** ACX "Who Predicted 2023?",
  https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/who-predicted-2023 (read, verbatim quotes checked): ~3,300 entrants, 50
  questions; Metaculus 99.5th percentile, participant aggregate 95th, Manifold 89th; "Of the 15 top-scoring 2022
  participants, 5 ... try again this year. On average, they scored in the 88th percentile". ACX "Who Predicted
  2022?" (workspace copy): 508-participant aggregate at the 84th percentile, 12-superforecaster aggregate at the 97th,
  and the winner "most likely ... won by a combination of skill and luck". Consistent with W1/W2, but n = 5 for
  the regression claim.
- **P4. Extremizing a single statistical model.** No evidence found. Theory (Neyman & Roughgarden) says the
  optimal extremizing factor is 1 when signals are fully correlated; one model is one signal.
- **P5. Calibration measures that are stable at small n.** Dimitriadis, Gneiting & Jordan, "Evaluating
  probabilistic classifiers: Reliability diagrams and score decompositions revisited", PNAS 118 (2021),
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03033 (abstract read): binned reliability diagrams lack "stability under unavoidable,
  ad hoc implementation decisions"; CORP uses isotonic (PAV) fitting, a Murphy decomposition that generalises to
  any proper score, and resampling or asymptotic uncertainty bands. Kumar, Liang & Ma, "Verified Uncertainty
  Calibration", NeurIPS 2019, https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155 (abstract read): plug-in binned calibration-error
  estimates are biased, and a debiased estimator needs about O(√B) rather than O(B) samples. Both are
  better-behaved than 10-bin ECE; neither has been shown to have usable power at n ≈ 31.
- **P6. LLM judgmental forecasters.** Halawi, Zhang et al., "Approaching Human-Level Forecasting with Language
  Models" (workspace copy): a retrieval-augmented LM system "nears the crowd aggregate ... and in some settings
  surpasses it". Paleka, Goel, Geiping & Tramèr, "Pitfalls in Evaluating Language Model Forecasters" (workspace
  copy): such claims are undermined by "many forms of temporal leakage". Any LLM overlay on this system would
  collide with the no-look-ahead rule: an LLM's weights were trained on the outcomes being backtested.
- **P7. Incremental updating as a design property.** The GJP finding (W3, Atanasov 2020) is about humans. That a
  model whose monthly probabilities move in small steps scores better than one that jumps at regime switches is
  plausible, but it was not tested for statistical models.

## Directly transferable to THIS system, ranked by expected gain

Constraint honoured throughout: the thresholds in experiment 0001 do not move. Anything below that changes the
model or the tests is a candidate for a **new pre-registered experiment**, adjudicated by Jeddy (E3), never a
retrospective amendment.

1. **Read the calibration-gate failure as uninformative at this n — do not "fix" it.** (Gain: prevents a wrong
   conclusion and a wasted build; cost: zero.) Derived in W5: at ~31 independent observations a *perfectly*
   calibrated forecaster fails 10-bin ECE < 0.10 about 91% of the time (median ECE 0.153). The verdict for the
   horizon stays SHIP BASE RATE, as registered. What should be added, as reporting rather than a rule change, is
   the ECE's null distribution at the measured effective n: simulate outcomes from the model's own probabilities,
   recompute ECE, and report where the observed value falls. That separates "miscalibrated" from "too few
   outcomes to tell". This is the same logic the module already applies to monotonicity reversals. For comparison,
   the SPF's ECE on ~230 quarterly outcomes is 0.05–0.07 (W7), so ECE only becomes informative in the low
   hundreds.
2. **Do not add post-hoc recalibration fitted on the backtest.** (Gain: avoids a likely loss.) At n ≈ 31, Platt,
   beta and isotonic raise out-of-sample Brier on average even when the model is miscalibrated in exactly Platt's
   form (W5). Fitting on the evaluation window would also be look-ahead. If a successor experiment wants
   recalibration, the only form the evidence supports is a walk-forward fit with a strong prior toward the identity
   map (ridge-Platt), with the penalty fixed in advance. Expected gain is about 0.002 Brier.
3. **Pre-register an equal-weight blend of the model with the expanding climatology as a successor.** (Gain:
   probably the largest real improvement available, size unknown until measured.) Evidence: every M-competition
   (W2); the equal-weights "combination puzzle" at small n; the M6 and yield-curve results showing the typical
   failure is overconfidence (M6 organisers; out-of-sample probit calibration slopes of 0.2–0.56 in W7). A fixed
   50/50 weight needs no estimation, so it cannot overfit. For the recession and yield-curve-inversion indicators,
   a three-way blend that adds the real-time spread probit is the natural extension.
4. **Add two external references to the report: the real-time spread probit and the SPF.** (Gain: context for the
   verdict; cost: one module, since the data are already public.) The honest bar at one year is that climatology
   is hard to beat: the famous yield-curve model's real-time BSS is −0.10 (1990–2025) and −0.41 (2006–2025) for
   "recession in month t+12", and professional forecasters have negative skill three to four quarters out (W7). A
   model that fails the skill gate is in good company; one that passes has beaten both.
5. **Report the log score, and each indicator's climatological Brier, next to BSS.** (Gain: diagnostic honesty
   on the rare and near-certain indicators.) Brier under-penalises confident errors on rare outcomes by roughly
   6× relative to the log score in the 5%-vs-1% case, and the mean BSS is dominated by lopsided indicators (W6).
   This is reporting, not a metric change.
6. **Measure month-to-month forecast volatility.** (Gain: small; plausible only, P7.) Filtered HMM probabilities
   can jump at regime switches. The GJP evidence favours small, frequent revisions. Worth a descriptive statistic
   before anything is built.

Not transferable: extremizing (P4 — there is no crowd to correct); superforecaster selection and teaming
(human-judgment effects); LLM overlays (P6 — incompatible with no-look-ahead).

Datasets (all fetched this run):
- SPF probabilities workbook (RECESS1–5 from 1968:Q4):
  https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/FRBP/Assets/Surveys-And-Data/survey-of-professional-forecasters/historical-data/prob.xlsx
- FRED CSV endpoint `https://fred.stlouisfed.org/graph/fredgraph.csv?id=<ID>` for GDPC1, USREC, GS10 and TB3MS
  (TB3MS is discount basis; the NY Fed uses bond-equivalent).
- NY Fed probit coefficients, fitted 1959–2005: α = −0.6045, β = −0.7374 on the 10y−3m spread (Estrella & Trubin 2006).

Formulas:
- Brier skill: BSS = 1 − BS_model / BS_climatology; BS_climatology = p(1−p) in expectation, so the denominator
  collapses for lopsided indicators (W6).
- Log-odds pooling with extremizing: logit p* = a · (1/N) Σ logit p_i. With N = 1 this is Baron's
  t(p) = p^a / (p^a + (1−p)^a) = σ(a · logit p). a > 1 extremizes and is justified only for partly independent
  forecasters (W4).
- Ridge-Platt: logit p′ = b + s · logit p, fitted by maximum likelihood with penalty λ[(s−1)² + b²], λ fixed in
  advance (λ = 5 in W5).
- Beta calibration: p′ = σ(a ln p − b ln(1−p) + c) (Kull et al. 2017).
- Fixed blend: p′ = w · p_model + (1−w) · p_climatology, w fixed in advance.
- ECE null test: draw y_i ~ Bernoulli(p_i) at the effective n, recompute ECE, report the observed value's
  percentile under perfect calibration.
